Bicing Prediction Models with Machine Learning

By Sharon Megan Wong, Diederik de Vries, Nupur Sircar and Siddharth Nambiar  ·  Published May 24, 2024

Bicing was launched in 2007 as a viable eco-friendly alternative or complement to traditional private and public transportation. The mobility network has allowed for increased physical activity and better connectivity between neighbourhoods of Barcelona. It is aimed at anyone with a NIE, over the age of 16. The Bicing 2.0 models were released in 2019 to include electrical charging docks. Currently, Smou is the mobile interface for Barcelona's mobility sharing. The forecast for availability of bicycles is limited to a future prediction within the next 15–30 minutes.

The task of this project was to predict the availability of Bicing bikes at any given station in Barcelona at any given time during the first three months of 2024, using machine learning algorithms in Python.

Availability

The investigation began by examining how availability had varied over time in the period 2019–2023.

The graph shows that the availability pattern deviated from typical behaviour in the first two years, 2019 and 2020. This can be attributed to a number of causes:

  • → 2019 — Bicing 2.0 launched alongside a new app that initially had many flaws, leading people to avoid using the service.
  • → 2020 — The Covid pandemic and lockdown caused a sharp dip, followed by a peak in August–October 2020 as residents chose Bicing over crowded public transport once restrictions lifted.

As a consequence of this analysis, the training data for the machine learning model was limited to the years 2021–2023.

Training Data

The model was trained on four distinct categories of data:

  • Spatial Latitude · Longitude · Altitude
  • Temporal Month · Day · Hour
  • Weather Temperature (Meteostat)
  • Supply & Demand Population density · Station capacity

Spatial and temporal data were retrieved from or produced from datasets given as defaults in the competition. Hourly temperature data was downloaded from the Meteostat Python library.

Population data was downloaded by censal neighbourhood from the Barcelona census. The number was then divided by the area of each neighbourhood to calculate population density. The maps below show (1) absolute population numbers and (2) population by area — an important distinction for understanding expected usage patterns.

Training Models

Three different machine learning models were tested, all based on the principle of the decision tree. A decision tree builds a prediction by dividing the dataset into halves across several iterations, based on logical patterns in the numerical values of the training data. Once constructed, a new dataset can be run through the model to predict availability based on other correlated inputs.

Results & Lessons Learned

Mean squared error was calculated across all three models, both on training data and against actual 2024 data:

0.064 Decision Tree Training MSE
0.023 Random Forest Training MSE
0.074 Gradient Booster Best on 2024 data ★

When compared to actual 2024 data, the gradient booster performed best at 0.074. This revealed that all three models were overfitted, giving an illusion of predictive precision. Below, the best prediction is plotted alongside the existing training data.

During peer presentations, two key lessons were identified for improving future predictions:

  1. Interrogate your data choices. The question should always be asked: why are we using the data that we are using? Understanding the correlation between chosen datasets and the target parameter would have allowed selection of data with higher predictive power. Some data added noise rather than signal. Concretely, a deeper understanding of how availability behaves in relation to spatial, temporal, weather, and supply/demand datasets would have helped identify which variables truly mattered.

  2. Motivate model choice with data patterns. The training data displayed nonlinear patterns in availability over the course of a day — a finding learned from peer presentations. This pattern motivates the use of a decision tree–based model over a linear regression variant, and should be a deliberate, reasoned choice rather than an incidental one.
